SUMMARY This paper proposes a Field-Programmable Gate Array
(FPGA) accelerator for Vision Transformers (ViTs) with quantization and
look-up-table (LUT) based operations. First, two improved quantization
methods are proposed, achieving comparable performance at lower bit-
widths. Furthermore, linear and nonlinear units’ designs are proposed to
support diverse operations in ViTs models. Finally, the LUT-based acceler-
ator design is implemented and evaluated. Experimental results on the Ima-
geNet dataset demonstrate that our proposed quantization method achieves
an accuracy of 80.74% at 2-bit width, outperforming state-of-the-art Vision
Transformer quantization methods by 0.1% to 0.5%. The performance of
the proposed FPGA accelerator demonstrates a higher energy eﬃciency,
achieving a peak energy eﬃciency of 7.06 FPS/W and 246 GOPS/W.
key words: vision transformer, FPGA, quantization, acceleration
1. Introduction
Over the past few years, the Transformer architecture has rev-
olutionized the ﬁeld of natural language processing (NLP).
Inspired by its success in NLP, Transformer-based models,
such as Vision Transformers (ViTs), have demonstrated re-
markable capabilities across various computer vision (CV)
tasks, often surpassing state-of-the-art convolutional neural
networks (CNNs) in performance [1]–[3]. These tasks in-
clude image classiﬁcation [4], [5], object detection [4], [6],
and image super-resolution [7].
However, ViTs are characterized by their complex
model structures, high computational demands, and sig-
niﬁcant memory requirements, often involving hundreds of
millions of parameters. For instance, the ViT-L model con-
tains approximately 307 million parameters, resulting in high
memory consumption and extended inference times. De-
ploying such models on resource-constrained edge devices
is a signiﬁcant challenge. Additionally, many real-time CV
applications demand low latency, making eﬃcient deploy-
ment even more critical. Consequently, model compression

Model quantization has been extensively studied as a
technique for reducing the computational complexity to ac-
celerate DNN inference on edge devices. Existing quantiza-
tion methods can be broadly categorized into post-training
quantization (PTQ), quantization-aware training (QAT) and
binaray quantization.
PTQ methods apply quantization to pre-trained models
without additional training, making them computationally
eﬃcient but often leading to accuracy degradation, especially
for low-bit quantization. PTQ4ViT [8] proposed similarity-
aware and ranking-aware quantization algorithms to ﬁnd the
optimal quantization interval of the MLP and self-attention
layers. TSPTQ-ViT [9] proposed two-scaled post-training
quantization scheme to reduce the quantization loss and im-
prove accuracy.
In contrast, QAT methods [10], [11] incorporate quan-
tization into the training process, allowing the model to
adapt to quantization eﬀect and achieve better accuracy.
FPQ4ViT [10] proposed a learning-based Quantization-
aware Training approach. Auto-ViT-Acc [11] proposed
mixed-scheme quantization algorithm to improve compu-
tation eﬃciency and maintian accuracy.
Recent studies have explored low-bit quantization tech-
niques for ViTs, aiming to improve eﬃciency while main-
taining model accuracy. Binary and ternary quantization
methods [12], [13] have been proposed to further reduce
model size and computational complexity. Full-binarized
ViTs[14] replace full-precision weights and activations with
binary values, enabling eﬃcient bitwise operations. How-
ever, these extreme quantization approaches often lead to sig-
niﬁcant accuracy degradation. Some studies [8], [11] have
investigated mixed-precision quantization, allocating diﬀer-
ent bit-widths to diﬀerent layers to balance eﬃciency and
accuracy.
While PTQ methods oﬀer a lightweight solution for
quantization without requiring retraining, they often strug-
gle with severe accuracy degradation under ultra-low-bit set-
tings due to the limited ﬂexibility in optimizing pre-trained
weights. QAT methods improve accuracy by incorporating
quantization into the training process, but they introduce ad-
ditional computational overhead and require careful tuning
to prevent unstable convergence. Full binary quantization
further reduces model size and computational complexity
but suﬀers from signiﬁcant performance loss, making it im-
practical for ViT-based applications with high accuracy re-

2. Preliminaries
This section provides an overview of the Vision Transformer
(ViT) architecture and various quantization schemes. A
detailed analysis of the ViT model components, particu-
larly the implementation of its nonlinear parts, is presented,
highlighting their importance in model inference. Further-
more, diﬀerent quantization techniques, including uniform
and non-uniform quantization intervals, are explored. By
comparing and analyzing these techniques, we reveal their
potential impact on resource utilization and computational
performance in FPGA implementations.
2.1 Vision Transformer
The Transformer model, initially designed for NLP tasks,
revolutionized the ﬁeld by addressing the limitations of
RNNs such as LSTMs and GRUs. Its encoder-decoder struc-
ture and self-attention mechanisms enable parallel computa-
tion, reducing training time and improving task performance,
particularly in machine translation. As depicted in Fig. 1, the
Vision Transformer (ViT) encoder comprisesL identical lay-
ers, each containing two primary components: a multi-head
Fig. 1 Vision Transformer structure.
self-attention (MSA) mechanism and a multi-layer percep-
tron (MLP). Layer normalization (LN) [16] is applied prior
to each sub-layer, and residual connections merge outputs
with the input to preserve information ﬂow.
ViT enhances feature extraction by employing multi-
head self-attention, where multiple attention heads process
data simultaneously, and their results are concatenated to
produce the ﬁnal output. The computational complexity of
ViT arises primarily from extensive matrix multiplication
operations in the MLP and MSA blocks, which dominate the
overall workload. While quantization strategies typically
target the linear layers to reduce computational demand, the
nonlinear layers, such as softmax, layer normalization and
GELU, which play a critical role in enhancing the model’s
expressive power, often remain in full precision due to their
lower cost and critical impact on model accuracy.

2.2 Quantization Schemes
Uniform interval quantization encompasses binary, ternary,
and ﬁxed-point approaches. Binary Neural Networks
(BNNs) [12] and XNOR-Net [13] exemplify binary quan-
tization, where restricting weights to (  1, +1) allows re-
placing multiplications with additions/subtractions. Ternary
quantization, by including zero, requires an additional bit
for weight representation but similarly beneﬁts from non-
multiplicative operations. While both binary and ternary
quantization signiﬁcantly reduce computational complexity
and simplify hardware accelerator implementations, they in-
cur a noticeable accuracy penalty.
In contrast, ﬁxed-point quantization employs moder-
ate and adaptable bit-widths, enabling the maintenance of
accuracy comparable to 32-bit ﬂoating-point models while
improving eﬃciency.

3.2 Accelerator Design
In FPGA design, consolidating multiple smaller-scale de-
signs into a single large-scale design can signiﬁcantly reduce
complexity while improving energy eﬃciency. The FPGA
computing array is typically implemented as a systolic ar-
ray comprising multiple computing units, parameterized by
using ( ni, no), where ni signiﬁes the input channels, and
no represents the output channels. To optimize the comput-
ing array conﬁguration, an exhaustive search Algorithm 2 is
proposed to aim at maximizing eﬀective throughput. This
method evaluated the actual throughput across diﬀerent ( ni,
no) conﬁgurations to identify the optimal solution. Specif-
ically, all possible ( ni, no) combinations are iterated for all
layers to calculate the total throughput. Under the quantiza-
tion scheme adopted in this work, the conﬁgurations are set
as follows: W2A6 – (54, 128), W4A8 – (64, 64), and W8A8
– (64, 32).
The proposed FPGA accelerator for Transformers is
illustrated in Fig. 4, and the structure of the Processing Ele-
ment is shown in Fig. 5. To enable both attention layers and
linear layer computations on a single large-scale computing
array, the design incorporates three types of processing el-
ements (PEs): PE0, PE1 and PE2. Each PE supports two
operational modes, corresponding to the computation re-
quirements of attention layers and linear layers, respectively.
During execution, preprocessed partitioned blocks are trans-
ferred from DRAM to either the input buﬀer or the weight
buﬀer. The computing array then retrieves data and weights
from these buﬀers for computation. The output buﬀer feeds
the data into the nonlinear acceleration unit. Once processed,
the data is either written back to DRAM or transferred to the
input buﬀer or weight buﬀer for subsequent computations.
